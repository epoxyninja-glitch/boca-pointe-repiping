Tell Us About Your Property

The material

What is polybutylene — and why does it get flagged?

Polybutylene, often called PB, is a plastic water-supply piping material used in some homes built from the late 1970s through the mid-1990s. Its history of premature failures and leaks is why it can become an issue during inspections and insurance reviews.

But a construction date does not confirm the material in your home. And identifying PB does not establish an exact failure date.

A qualified plumber should evaluate the accessible piping, explain the findings, and discuss the appropriate next steps.

The assessment

Start with findings — not a sales pitch.

The assessment is intended to answer:

  • What supply-pipe materials can be identified?
  • Is there evidence of previous replacement or partial repairs?
  • What portions of the system are accessible?
  • What remains concealed or unconfirmed?
  • If replacement is recommended, what work would it include?

You receive an explanation of the findings and, where appropriate, a written replacement proposal.

Assessment scope, any visit fee, and written deliverables will be confirmed when scheduling. Concealed piping may require additional investigation.

The proposal

A repiping estimate should explain more than the pipe.

Before approving work, you should know what will be replaced — and what happens to your home during the process.

Your written proposal should address:

  • Supply lines and fixture connections included in the work.
  • Proposed replacement material.
  • Access and routing.
  • Wall or ceiling openings.
  • Drywall, texture, painting, and other restoration responsibilities.
  • Permit and inspection responsibilities.
  • Expected water interruptions.
  • Warranty terms.
  • Exclusions and separately priced work.

Attached residences

Own a condo or townhome? Define the project boundaries first.

For an attached residence, the proposal should identify the piping being evaluated, the proposed access, and any questions that need to be resolved with management or the association.

Before work is scheduled, clarify:

  • What work is within your residence.
  • Whether shared areas or building systems may be affected.
  • What approvals or access arrangements are needed.
  • How water interruptions and work hours will be coordinated.
  • Who is responsible for each part of the project.

Do not assume that a quote for one residence covers a shared building system.

Questions

Short answers.

Can you tell whether I have polybutylene from my address?

No. An address or construction year can guide research, but it does not establish what piping is currently installed. Renovations and partial replacements must also be considered.

Can I send a photograph?

Yes. A clear image of safely accessible piping or printed markings may help with preliminary identification, but it does not document the entire concealed system. Do not cut pipes, remove walls, or disturb plumbing to take a photograph.

Does an inspection report replace an on-site assessment?

It provides useful background. The contractor should still establish the scope needed to prepare an accurate proposal.

Does repiping include drain lines?

The proposal must say. Water-supply replacement and drain-line work should not be treated as interchangeable scopes.

Will the walls be repaired afterward?

Check the written proposal. It should identify who handles patching, texture, painting, and any other restoration — and what is excluded.

Will replacement satisfy my insurer?

Ask your insurer or agent for its specific requirements. The contractor can describe the proposed work and available completion documentation, but should not promise an underwriting decision.
